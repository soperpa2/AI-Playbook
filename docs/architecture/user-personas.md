# User Personas

## How to use these personas

These are design and content-governance tools grounded in the roles, tasks, learning, governance, workspace, and contribution capabilities visible on the current site. They are hypotheses to validate through interviews and task testing, not stereotypes. Agency size, jurisdiction, authority, resources, accessibility needs, and AI experience may matter more than job title.

For each persona, role pathways should curate existing canonical content and relationships rather than duplicate it.

## 1. Health Officer / Executive Sponsor — “Accountable decision-maker”

**Context:** Responsible for mission, public trust, resources, legal authority, and executive decisions; limited time and uneven technical depth.  
**Goals:** Set vision and guardrails; understand readiness; establish accountable governance; approve a realistic portfolio and funding path; see risks and evidence quickly.  
**Pain points:** AI hype, fragmented pilots, unclear ownership, technical detail without decisions, unsupported benefit claims.  
**Key tasks:** Plays 1–3 and 7–9; readiness/maturity interpretation; executive learning; decision briefs; governance and funding tools; credible Project Summaries.  
**Needs from platform:** A 10-minute orientation, decision-focused summaries, evidence/freshness labels, clear required actions, printable briefings, and organization progress at an appropriate aggregate level.  
**Success:** Can explain what is permitted, who decides, what comes next, and why evidence supports the chosen path.

## 2. Epidemiologist — “Evidence and surveillance practitioner”

**Context:** Works with high-volume, sensitive, time-dependent data and is accountable for scientific validity and interpretation.  
**Goals:** Reduce analytical burden, identify useful signals, validate methods, protect confidentiality, and preserve human investigation judgment.  
**Pain points:** Poor data quality, opaque models, false alerts, non-representative training data, weak evaluation, pressure to automate conclusions.  
**Key tasks:** Find surveillance and outbreak examples; use data quality, validation, monitoring, equity, and incident tools; learn predictive/ML concepts; examine sources.  
**Needs:** Technical depth on demand, data and outcome specificity, evaluation limitations, reproducible citations, human-oversight patterns, and downloadable checklists.  
**Success:** Can assess whether a proposed workflow is scientifically and operationally fit for a bounded pilot.

## 3. Informatics Director — “Bridge between programs, data, and governance”

**Context:** Coordinates data modernization, interoperability, architecture, governance, analytics, and program requirements.  
**Goals:** Align AI work with existing infrastructure and governance; identify dependencies; create reusable patterns; prevent isolated solutions.  
**Pain points:** Duplicated systems, unclear data ownership, inconsistent metadata, vendor lock-in, pilots without sustainment.  
**Key tasks:** Readiness and maturity assessment; architecture/data tools; use-case portfolio; implementation sequencing; crosswalks; organization planning.  
**Needs:** Relationship views, dependencies, standards mappings, machine-readable exports, reference architecture, and content version history.  
**Success:** Can map an approved use case to data, governance, workforce, funding, and implementation requirements.

## 4. Program Manager — “Outcome and workflow owner”

**Context:** Owns services, grants, staff workflows, performance, and partner commitments; may have limited technical capacity.  
**Goals:** Solve a specific operational problem, evaluate fit, plan change, secure resources, and measure public health outcomes.  
**Pain points:** Abstract AI language, tools detached from workflow, hidden staff burden, unclear procurement path, pilots that do not scale.  
**Key tasks:** Submit and score use cases; create charter and implementation plan; map workflow/stakeholders; use Project Summaries; assign actions and learning.  
**Needs:** Guided paths, practical templates, examples by domain, plain language, effort estimates, and explicit handoffs to governance/IT.  
**Success:** Produces a review-ready use case with owners, measures, risks, change needs, and sustainment assumptions.

## 5. IT / Technical Lead — “Secure implementer and operator”

**Context:** Evaluates architecture, integration, identity, security, vendors, deployment, monitoring, support, and incident response.  
**Goals:** Translate approved needs into reliable bounded systems; establish controls; avoid unsafe data flows; operate sustainably.  
**Pain points:** Unclear requirements, shadow AI, missing threat models, weak vendor transparency, no monitoring or exit criteria.  
**Key tasks:** Technical readiness, data classification, vendor/security review, validation, pilot design, monitoring, incident and retirement planning.  
**Needs:** Detailed implementation artifacts, control mappings, data-flow expectations, versioned requirements, machine-readable relationships, and clear human approval boundaries.  
**Success:** Can implement and support a solution whose access, data, failure modes, auditability, and rollback are understood.

## 6. Communications Professional — “Trusted public translator”

**Context:** Creates staff, partner, media, emergency, and public information under accuracy, accessibility, language-access, and trust constraints.  
**Goals:** Use AI for bounded drafting and adaptation while preserving factual, cultural, and organizational review.  
**Pain points:** Hallucinations, inaccessible or culturally weak language, unclear disclosure, inconsistent approval, speed pressure.  
**Key tasks:** Learn generative AI and source verification; use communication/change tools; find language-access Project Summaries; document human review.  
**Needs:** Approved-use examples, review checklists, prompt/data boundaries, language and accessibility metadata, versioned source material, disclosure guidance.  
**Success:** Publishes timely, accessible, verified content with documented subject-matter and communications approval.

## 7. Researcher / Evaluator — “Evidence builder”

**Context:** Designs studies, evaluates pilots, synthesizes literature, and distinguishes claims from evidence.  
**Goals:** Find citable sources and comparable implementations; assess validity and equity; define measures; contribute findings.  
**Pain points:** Vendor claims presented as evidence, missing methods, stale links, ambiguous case status, non-exportable metadata.  
**Key tasks:** Browse references and cases by evidence tier/capability/domain; inspect outcomes and limitations; use evaluation tools; export citations/data; propose corrections.  
**Needs:** Provenance, claim-source linkage, last verification, evidence status, stable citations, structured export, and clear synthesis disclosures.  
**Success:** Can reproduce the evidence trail and design an evaluation that tests benefits, harms, workflow, equity, and sustainment.

## 8. Student / Learner — “Guided newcomer”

**Context:** May be a student, new employee, community learner, or practitioner building AI literacy independently.  
**Goals:** Understand terms and responsible-use basics; connect concepts to public health work; practice safely; know when to escalate.  
**Pain points:** Jargon, too many entry points, assumed technical knowledge, fear or overconfidence, exercises without feedback.  
**Key tasks:** Choose a pathway; complete modules and practical exercises; use glossary; explore examples; save progress where appropriate.  
**Needs:** Clear prerequisites, time estimates, learning objectives, low-risk examples, formative feedback disclaimers, accessibility, and visible progress without blocking public access.  
**Success:** Can explain a bounded use case, major risks, required human review, and the next role to involve.

## 9. Governance, Privacy, Legal, Security, or Risk Lead — “Control and accountability steward”

**Why this additional persona is high value:** Governance is central across current plays, tools, readiness, case presentations, and organization workflows.  
**Goals:** Establish policy and decision rights; apply risk-tiered review; document approvals; monitor incidents and changing requirements.  
**Pain points:** Governance added after procurement, incomplete inventories, unclear authority, untraceable claims, overlapping reviews.  
**Key tasks:** Plays 1–3, 7, and 12; review sources and applicability; manage inventory, risk, approvals, monitoring, and retirement.  
**Needs:** Jurisdiction/freshness metadata, auditable templates, relationship traceability, risk filters, version history, and exportable records.  
**Success:** Can determine status, owner, required review, evidence, and next decision for every AI-supported workflow.

## 10. Equity & Community Engagement Lead / Community Partner — “Missing-voices advocate”

**Why high value:** The site repeatedly emphasizes stakeholder engagement, equity review, community accountability, language access, and Tribal data sovereignty.  
**Goals:** Identify affected communities, surface harms and access barriers, shape design early, and see how input changed decisions.  
**Pain points:** Engagement after decisions, technical framing, extractive consultation, inaccessible materials, weak feedback loops.  
**Key tasks:** Stakeholder mapping; equity and accessibility review; inspect community-impacting cases; contribute feedback; track mitigations.  
**Needs:** Plain-language and multilingual materials, accessible participation, explicit data/use boundaries, decision logs, community-visible corrections, and appropriate Tribal governance.  
**Success:** Community input has documented influence and unresolved concerns reach an accountable decision-maker.

## 11. Procurement / Finance / Grants Lead — “Commercial and sustainability gatekeeper”

**Why high value:** Funding strategy, grants, vendor review, implementation, and sustainment are explicit current-site tasks.  
**Goals:** Evaluate total cost, allowable funding, contract controls, vendor claims, data rights, accessibility, auditability, and exit options.  
**Pain points:** License-only budgets, vague AI features, non-negotiable terms, unsupported ROI, no sustainment or transition plan.  
**Key tasks:** Plays 7–9 and 11; funding portfolio; vendor/procurement tools; compare examples; document approvals and obligations.  
**Needs:** Requirement checklists, cost categories, evidence tiers, contract/control mappings, source dates, and implementation dependencies.  
**Success:** A purchase or grant plan covers responsible implementation, evaluation, operations, and exit—not only acquisition.

## 12. Workforce / Training Lead or AI Champion — “Adoption enabler”

**Why high value:** The current site contains role-based learning, assignments, training tracking concepts, change planning, and AI champions.  
**Goals:** Build role-appropriate literacy, support behavior change, address concerns, and measure readiness and completion.  
**Pain points:** One-size training, no protected time, learning detached from projects, completion mistaken for competence.  
**Key tasks:** Plays 5, 6, and 10; assign pathways; curate modules and exercises; run office hours; monitor barriers and feedback.  
**Needs:** Role mappings, prerequisites, learning outcomes, revision-aware assignments, accessible exports, and aggregate progress with privacy limits.  
**Success:** People can perform their responsibilities safely and know where to obtain help or escalate concerns.

## 13. Organization Administrator — “Workspace coordinator”

**Why high value:** My Account and Organization Hub currently envision profiles, plans, assignments, trackers, and exports.  
**Goals:** Configure the organization, coordinate plans and training, manage permissions, and produce status summaries.  
**Pain points:** Unclear role boundaries, duplicated data, sensitive results exposed too broadly, manual reporting.  
**Key tasks:** Manage membership/roles, connect canonical plays/tools to workplans, assign training, export status, handle offboarding.  
**Needs:** Least-privilege access, tenancy boundaries, audit history, revision-aware content references, consent/retention controls, and support.  
**Success:** Coordinates work without becoming an unrestricted data administrator or duplicating the public content library.

## Cross-persona design priorities

1. Offer a role-and-task entry point without hiding the full platform.
2. Show evidence type, implementation status, source, and review date consistently.
3. Let users move from explanation → example → play → tool → learning → evidence.
4. Keep public content open; ask for an account only when persistence or collaboration creates clear value.
5. Validate with agency size and jurisdiction variants, especially local, Tribal, territorial, and resource-constrained organizations.
6. Measure task success and confidence, not simply page views or course completion.

## Research plan

Recruit at least two participants from each primary persona cluster (executive/governance, program/practice, technical/data, communications/community, learning/research), with intentional variation in agency size and jurisdiction. Test: selecting a starting path, finding a relevant tool, judging a Project Summary’s evidence, tracing a source, planning a use case, and understanding account/workspace boundaries. Revise these personas after each research round and record evidence supporting changes.
